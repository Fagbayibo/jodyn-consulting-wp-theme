Many organisations cut training budgets first when times are hard. Yet the cost of poor leadership, through missed targets, disengaged staff and high turnover, is often far greater. This article explains why leadership training should be treated as an investment and how to measure its return.

## The Hidden Cost of Unprepared Leaders

When a manager is promoted without preparation, the cost rarely shows up as a single line in the budget. It shows up as slower decisions, missed deadlines and good people quietly looking for the exit.

- **Turnover:** people leave managers, not companies, and every replacement costs time and money: recruiting, onboarding and months before the new person is fully productive.
- **Low engagement:** teams that are unclear on priorities do the minimum, and the best performers notice first.
- **Poor decisions:** without a framework, managers decide by instinct, or put decisions off until they become problems.
- **Slow execution:** work stalls when everything has to pass through one overloaded person.

None of these appears on a training invoice, which is why they are easy to overlook. But each one reduces what the organisation delivers, every month the gap remains.

## Why Promotions Alone Do Not Create Leaders

The skills that make someone excellent at their job are not the skills they need to lead others. The best accountant is not automatically the best head of finance; the top salesperson is not automatically the best sales manager.

### From doing the work to leading the work

The shift is from personal output to team output: setting direction, removing obstacles and growing the people around you. Without support, many new managers fall back on what they know best, doing the work themselves, and the team waits for them. Others swing the other way and step back so far that the team loses direction.

New managers also face a problem that rarely gets named: they now lead people who were their peers last month. Setting expectations for former colleagues, and holding them to account, is uncomfortable without guidance on how to do it well.

## What Effective Leadership Training Changes

Good programmes change behaviour, not just knowledge. Managers learn to set clear expectations, delegate well and hold the conversations they used to avoid, and they practise those skills before they use them with their teams.

In practice, that looks like:

- **Clearer priorities:** managers can explain what matters most this quarter, and why, so their teams spend time on the right work.
- **Better delegation:** work is handed over with the context and authority to finish it, not just the task.
- **Regular feedback:** problems are raised early and in private, instead of building up until the annual review.
- **Steadier decisions:** managers use a simple, shared way of weighing options, so decisions are faster and easier to explain.

## Signs Your Managers Need Support

Leadership gaps tend to show up in the same places. It may be time to invest if you notice:

- one or two teams with much higher staff turnover than the rest;
- managers who are always busy but whose teams are often waiting on them;
- the same problems coming back to senior leaders instead of being solved a level below;
- newly promoted managers who are struggling, months after the promotion;
- engagement or pulse-survey scores falling in particular teams.

## How to Measure the Return on Leadership Training

Training is only an investment if you can see what it returns. Agree a few simple measures before the programme starts, then track them again three and six months later.

1. Team engagement or pulse-survey scores
2. Staff turnover in the manager's team
3. Delivery against agreed targets
4. Feedback from the manager's own team

Keep the list short and choose measures you already collect. The aim is not a perfect calculation; it is a clear before-and-after picture that tells you whether the programme is changing how your managers lead. It also helps to ask each participant to set one or two personal goals at the start, and to check them with their own manager afterwards.

## What to Look for in a Leadership Programme

Not every programme delivers lasting change. Look for one that is:

- **Practical:** built around real situations your managers face, with time to practise.
- **Tailored:** adjusted to your sector, your culture and the level of your managers.
- **Measurable:** clear about the outcomes it aims for, and how they will be tracked.
- **Followed up:** supported after the classroom, through coaching, check-ins or a follow-up session.

## Key takeaways

- Poor leadership costs more than training: turnover, low engagement and slow execution add up.
- A promotion is not preparation: new managers need support to lead people.
- Good programmes change what managers do, not only what they know.
- Agree simple measures before the programme and track them afterwards.
- Choose programmes that are practical, tailored, measurable and followed up.
