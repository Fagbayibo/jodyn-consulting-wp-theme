Most strategies fail in the doing, not the planning. This article shows how to turn a good plan into clear priorities, named owners and results you can track.

## The Gap Between Planning and Doing

Many organisations put real effort into their strategy: a leadership retreat, a well-designed document, a presentation to staff. Then daily work takes over. Six months later, the strategy sits in a folder and the year looks much like the last one.

This is rarely because the strategy was wrong. It is because nobody translated it into what each team should do differently on Monday morning.

## Five Reasons Good Plans Fail

### 1. Too many priorities

When everything is a priority, nothing is. A strategy with fifteen goals spreads attention, money and people too thinly to move any of them far. Teams pick the goals that suit them, and the ones that matter most may be left behind.

### 2. No clear owners

Goals assigned to "management" or "everyone" belong to no one. Without a named person accountable for each priority, progress depends on goodwill, and goodwill fades when people are busy.

### 3. Strategy that never reaches the front line

Senior leaders may understand the strategy well, while the people doing the work have never seen it, or cannot connect it to their own jobs. If staff cannot explain how their work contributes, they cannot make choices that support it.

### 4. Nothing is measured

A goal such as "improve customer service" cannot be tracked. Without a few clear measures, nobody knows whether the strategy is working until the year is over, and by then it is too late to adjust.

### 5. No rhythm of review

Strategies are often reviewed once a year, at the next retreat. In between, the world changes: markets shift, costs rise, people leave. Without regular check-ins, the plan and reality drift apart.

## How to Turn a Plan into Results

### Choose three to five priorities

Narrow the strategy down to the few things that will make the biggest difference this year. For each, write one sentence on why it matters. Anything that does not make the list is still worth doing well, but it does not get extra time and money at the expense of the priorities.

### Name one owner for each

Every priority needs one person accountable for it: someone senior enough to make decisions and pull in resources, who reports on progress. Others will contribute, but one person owns the result.

### Break priorities into quarterly goals

A year is too long to manage. Break each priority into what must be achieved in the next three months, then agree what each team will contribute. This is where the strategy reaches the front line: each team should be able to point to its part.

### Agree a few measures

For each priority, choose two or three measures that show whether it is moving: for example, revenue from a new market, time taken to serve a customer, or the number of staff trained. Keep them simple, and use information you can collect without a major new system.

### Set a review rhythm

Hold a short monthly review of the priorities, led by their owners: what has moved, what is stuck, and what decision is needed. Each quarter, step back and ask whether the priorities themselves still make sense. A strategy that adapts is far more useful than one that is followed blindly.

## Communicating the Strategy

People support what they understand. Explain the strategy in plain language, more than once, through more than one channel. Show each team where its work fits. Celebrate progress against the priorities, so people can see the strategy is real. When something changes, explain why.

Managers are the most important link. Give them the time and tools to translate the strategy for their teams, and to answer questions honestly, including the ones they find difficult.

## Where to Start

If your strategy has stalled, you do not need a new one. Start by reviewing the current plan: pick the priorities that still matter, give each an owner, and set the first monthly review. Momentum comes from progress, however small.

## Key takeaways

- Strategies usually fail in execution, not planning.
- Focus on three to five priorities, each with one named owner.
- Break priorities into quarterly goals that every team can see its part in.
- Track a few simple measures and review progress monthly.
- Managers are the link between the strategy and the front line: equip them.
